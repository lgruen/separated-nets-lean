Beyond the cube

A point set with one point in each cube of a grid cannot do much better than the grid itself, and in the column picture of page 1 it cannot beat it at all. This page shows why, and the two ways the paper gets past the cube value 1 with only q3 points: two coordinates that share a digit, and three coordinates whose wraps are locked together. A last section does the same in dimension five with a half-integer layer.

Why columns cannot beat the cube

In a column point set every point sits on the diagonal of its cell, at a height that depends on its column. Two neighbouring columns are close when their heights differ by about 2/3 of a period: then their points are only √(2/3) cells apart. If g is how far the height steps stay from 2/3 of a period, the minimum distance is δN1/3 = √(2/3 + 3g2), measured in periods.

To beat the cube value 1 you need g > 1/3, so every step would have to lie strictly between 0 and 1/3 of a period. But three steps, along ex, then ey, then ez, lead back to the same column, so they add up to a whole number of periods, and three numbers between 0 and 1/3 cannot do that. The cube ℤ3 (steps 0) and its half-turn about (1,1,1) (steps 1/3) sit exactly on the ceiling. Move the first two steps and watch the third follow.

Two escapes at m = 3: shared and locked wraps

In the two-coset net 𝒯p, y and z read the same geometric digit, so they always wrap together, while x reads h(ny + nz) with h ≡ −1/2: neighbouring cells alternate between two heights half a cell apart. The slabs nx = const are puckered square grids, sheared by 1/8 from one slab to the next, and the two kinds of contact balance at the squared length 33/32. A coordinate that can wrap alone cannot move by 1 + α with a small α ≠ 0 along its own axis, because where it wraps alone the same step moves it by α only. So the free coordinate x moves by exactly one cell along ex, up to the jitter.

In the rank-one net 𝒲p, z reads a digit ζ and x and y both read 2ζ. So z wraps only where x and y wrap too: all three wraps are locked. That buys room for a drift: the step along ez is (4ε, 4ε, 1 + 2ε) with ε = ρ/(2p) close to 1/48, longer than one cell, and where z wraps it is still long because x and y wrap with it. The slab view puts the coordinate in question vertical, joins each point to the one in the next cell up, and colours the steps by how the second digits wrap. The third option shifts the second digit of z by 1, which unlocks the wraps (page 2, section 4).

How far the locked layer can go

Without the third digits, the value of 𝒲p is set by two contacts: the diagonal contact, whose squared length qa(ε) = 5/4 − 9ε + 81ε2 falls as the drift ε grows, and the step along ez, with qb(ε) = 1 + 4ε + 36ε2, which rises. They balance at ε* = (13 − 2√31)/90, where both equal μ*2. Among all rank-one layers with image (2, 2, 1), μ* is the least upper bound of the value without the third digits, and since ε* is irrational no prime attains it; 𝒲p aims at ε = 1/48, just past the balance, and its limit √281/16 lies just below μ*.

Dimension five: a half-integer layer

In dimension five the net 𝒬*p uses a layer whose entries are all half-integers, read modulo p through 1/2 ≡ (p + 1)/2. Up to the drift and the jitter, the point of cell n then sits at n or at n + (1/2, …, 1/2), according to the parity of n1 + … + n5: locally the packing D5+, with one point per unit cube and minimum √5/2 = 1.118…. The slab shows one plane of cells; the colour gives the parity.

Odd sums of half-integers can never be whole, so every step to a neighbouring cell with an odd number of moving coordinates keeps all five coordinates about half a cell long. The drift shortens them a little: δN1/5 ≈ (√5/2)(1 − 17/(5p)). The paper certifies δN1/5 > 1 for every prime p ≥ 37 with a relaxation that allows every wrap of the first two offset digits. The browser computes exact distances only while N = p5 stays small; beyond that the chart shows the certified bound.

How this page is checked

All nets are built by netcore.js, which the three pages share. A Python reference rebuilds 𝒯p, 𝒲p and 𝒬*p from the generator rows printed in the paper and compares point sets, the net property (Niederreiter's rank test and direct box counts), and exact minimum distances, both with an independent kernel and with the paper's own precomputed values. The bounds and the relaxation LB2 are recomputed from the paper's formulas and from its scripts. Distances are on the torus; decimals are truncated, except upper bounds, which are rounded up.
